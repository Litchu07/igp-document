Crisis 1 · Root Cause

The Policy Trap

The Green Revolution was an emergency measure that became a permanent cage. Launched in crisis — India faced famine and dependence on US PL-480 "ship-to-mouth" aid — Borlaug's high-yielding wheat varieties and Swaminathan's implementation delivered a genuine miracle. But the policy architecture built around that miracle locked farmers into an ever-deepening dependency.

Minimum Support Prices rewarded only wheat and rice. Fertiliser subsidies made chemical inputs cheaper than soil restoration. Electricity subsidies encouraged groundwater extraction at any depth. The result: farmers who wanted to grow anything else — lentils, oilseeds, vegetables — faced a structural penalty. By 1985, Punjab grew wheat and rice almost exclusively.

1947–65 · Pre-Revolution
Pesticide use: 154 metric tonnes. India imports 10 million tonnes of grain annually. "Ship-to-mouth" dependency. B.P. Pal introduces Lerma Rojo wheat variety.
1966–68 · The Revolution
Borlaug's HYV wheat introduced. Punjab and Haryana adopted first. Production explodes: wheat output triples by 1972. India declares food self-sufficiency. Swaminathan and Borlaug win awards — but Swaminathan privately warns of ecological recklessness.
1970s · The Lock-In
MSP framework formalised. Fertiliser subsidy regime creates input dependency. Rice added to the wheat-only Green Revolution. Punjab water table begins dropping — 1 metre/year by 1975. Pesticide use reaches 10,000 MT.
1980–2000 · The Treadmill
Pest resistance emerges; farmers spray more to get the same effect. Pesticide use peaks at ~88,000 MT (1994–2000). Punjab uses 923 g/ha — highest in India. Fertiliser efficiency collapses from 50 kg grain/kg NPK to under 10 kg. First cancer clusters reported in Malwa.
2000–2025 · The Reckoning
Cancer Train documented. BARC identifies uranium in Punjab groundwater. 3,300 farmer suicides recorded. Water table at 300 metres in places. Four pesticide reform bills fail in Parliament. India's national cancer burden rises; Punjab ranks 5th in absolute cases, 3rd in mortality.

"The green revolution has been a miserable failure from the social and economic standpoint... we are rapidly approaching the end of the era of the green revolution."

— M.S. Swaminathan, 1968 internal note (declassified 1990s)
Crisis 2 · Ecological

The Death of the Soil

Green Revolution monocultures extracted nutrients at a pace soil biology cannot replenish. Continuous wheat-rice cycles eliminated the legume rotations that fixed nitrogen naturally. Chemical fertilisers fed crops but starved soil microbiomes. Within two decades of intensive cultivation, Punjab's soils began dying from the inside.

The micronutrient collapse has made food less nutritious even as it made it more abundant. Zinc deficiency now affects paddy yields; sulfur deficiency is endemic. Farmers apply more fertiliser each season simply to maintain yields that no longer respond — the classic input treadmill.

In 2015, soil scientists from PAU (Punjab Agricultural University) estimated that restoring Punjab's organic carbon levels to the 1966 baseline would require abandoning monocultures for at least fifteen years — a transition no farmer dependent on MSP-priced wheat and rice can afford.

Biodiversity Lost

Before 1966, Punjab supported 300+ traditional wheat varieties, each adapted to local microclimates. By 1990, three HYV varieties covered 95% of cultivated area. The genetic bottleneck left the crop uniquely vulnerable to new rust strains — prompting yet more fungicide use. The wild relatives of wheat that once served as gene banks were ploughed under.

Crisis 3 · Hydrological

The Poisoned Aquifer

Punjab's water emergency has two dimensions: quantity and quality. On quantity, paddy cultivation requires 1,200–1,500 mm of water per season in a state receiving 600–700 mm of rainfall — the difference comes entirely from groundwater. On quality, decades of agrochemical application have turned that groundwater into a toxic cocktail.

The uranium contamination is linked not to industrial discharge but to phosphate fertilisers — a direct consequence of Green Revolution inputs. Phosphate rock naturally contains uranium; when dissolved by soil acids and concentrated in groundwater by evapotranspiration, it creates a slow radiological poison that no filtration system in rural Punjab removes.

On the Sutlej river, PGIMER studies found elevated cadmium, lead, and mercury — heavy metals from agrochemical manufacturing effluents and pesticide residues. The Ghaggar carries DDT and HCH (hexachlorocyclohexane) at 90%+ above WHO thresholds. These rivers irrigate the fields that feed the rest of India.

A Nationwide Water Emergency

Punjab is the extreme case, but the pattern repeats across Green Revolution states. Haryana's groundwater is critically overexploited in 80% of assessed blocks. Western UP and coastal Andhra Pradesh show similar agrochemical contamination profiles. NITI Aayog's 2018 Composite Water Management Index ranked these states in the highest-stress bracket.

India extracts more groundwater annually than the United States and China combined — largely to sustain paddy cultivation in water-scarce states. The Green Revolution transferred production to where water was, then mined it.

Crisis 4 · Human Health

The Health Catastrophe

The health burden of Green Revolution chemicalisation is not limited to cancer — though cancer is the crisis most visible to the outside world. Neurological disorders, birth defects, hormonal disruption, and reproductive harm are documented across pesticide-intensive farming communities. Cancer is the signal, but the damage is systemic.

Cancer

The Cancer Belt

Malwa region: 1,089 cancer cases/million vs national 800/million. Punjab ranks 5th in absolute cancer cases nationally and 3rd in cancer mortality. PGIMER studies find 125 cancer cases/100,000 in pesticide-heavy blocks vs 72/100,000 in comparison areas.

Neurological

Neurological Harm

Organophosphate pesticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase — the enzyme regulating nerve signals. Chronic low-dose exposure linked to Parkinson's disease, cognitive decline, and depression. Punjab and Haryana report elevated rates; farmers handling monocrotophos and chlorpyrifos show measurable neurological impairment.

Reproductive

Birth Defects & Hormonal Disruption

Endocrine-disrupting pesticides mimic oestrogen. Studies in Malwa link pesticide exposure to early puberty, increased spontaneous abortions, and birth defects. A 2025 study found 6.7× higher ovarian cancer odds in women with high organophosphate exposure.

Mental Health

Pesticide Poisoning & Suicide

India accounts for 6,600 of 11,000 global annual accidental pesticide deaths. Intentional poisoning is the leading suicide method among farmers. In Malwa, organophosphate poisoning is so common that local hospitals stock atropine as a routine emergency supply.

One Symptom Among Many

The Cancer Train (Train No. 14796/97)

Every night at 9:30 PM, a train departs Bathinda railway station for Bikaner in Rajasthan — carrying roughly 100 cancer patients and their caregivers to the Acharya Tulsi Regional Cancer Treatment and Research Institute, one of the few facilities that provides subsidised treatment. The train became a symbol after journalist Aman Sethi documented it in 2008. But the train is not the crisis — it is a coping mechanism. The crisis is in the fields, the water, and the policy architecture that created it.

Crisis 5 · Economic

Economic Ruin and the Farmer Suicide Crisis

The Green Revolution created two kinds of poverty in Punjab. The first is the debt trap: input costs for seeds, fertilisers, and pesticides now routinely exceed farm revenues in bad years, forcing farmers to borrow at usurious rates from informal lenders. The second is the health bankruptcy: a single cancer diagnosis can consume a family's accumulated wealth and leave it destitute.

Jajjal village in Bathinda district recorded roughly 55 cancer deaths over eleven years in a population of a few hundred families — a cluster so stark it attracted national attention. Widows in these villages describe a sequence: husband sprays pesticides for twenty years, develops cancer, treatment costs exhaust savings and land, family sells livestock and jewellery, husband dies, widow inherits debt and farmland she cannot cultivate alone.

"In our village, every family has either a cancer patient or a farm suicide. We cannot afford to treat, and we cannot afford to stop farming. There is no choice left."

— A farmer's widow in Bathinda district, interviewed by Kheti Virasat Mission field workers, 2019

The Youth Exodus

Punjab's youth migration crisis — the state has the highest per capita emigration rate in India — is directly linked to agricultural despair. Young men who grew up watching their fathers die in cancer wards or from suicide have little appetite for farming. The paradox: as Punjab loses its farming generation, the fields that remain are tilled by increasingly older farmers who continue the same input-intensive practices because no transition support exists.

Crisis 6 · Governance

Corporate Capture and Regulatory Failure

Behind the chemical treadmill is an industry that profits from it and a regulatory system that has not been meaningfully updated in over fifty years. The Insecticides Act 1968 — enacted the year after the Green Revolution took hold — remains the primary law governing pesticides. Four reform bills (2008, 2017, 2020, 2025) have failed in Parliament. The pesticide industry accounts for over 50% of India's agri-export revenue, giving it structural leverage over both the Ministry of Agriculture and the Ministry of Commerce.

Way Forward

Breaking the Treadmill

There is no single fix. The crises documented here are systemic — they require changes at the policy, regulatory, community, and knowledge levels simultaneously. But evidence-based models already exist and are working at scale in several Indian states.

Reform the MSP Architecture

Extend MSP support to pulses, oilseeds, and vegetables — rewarding crop diversity rather than monocultures. The Swaminathan Commission recommended this in 2004; it has not been implemented.

Zero-Budget Natural Farming

Andhra Pradesh's ZBNF programme reached 700,000+ farmers by 2022, reducing pesticide costs by 35% while maintaining yields. Subhash Palekar's model uses local cow-dung bio-inputs, eliminating external chemical inputs entirely.

Modernise the Insecticides Act

Pass the long-delayed Pesticide Management Bill. Adopt EU hazard classification standards. Mandate multilingual labelling in all states. Establish an independent toxicological review board free from industry nominees.

Soil Carbon Restoration

PAU's Living Soil programme targets organic carbon restoration through green manuring, biochar, and reduced tillage. Government incentives currently favour synthetic fertiliser over soil-building — reversing this subsidy structure is the critical first step.

Kheti Virasat Mission

Umendra Dutt's KVM has transitioned 30,000+ Punjab farmers to natural farming. The model integrates soil health, water conservation, and community savings to break the debt-input cycle simultaneously.

Universal Cancer Coverage

AAP's Punjab Cancer Relief Fund covered 23,711 patients with ₹305+ crore in 2022–23. But downstream relief cannot substitute for upstream prevention. Without addressing the chemical causes, the treatment burden will grow faster than relief budgets can cover.

The Green Revolution is not the past — it is the present. Punjab's farmers are still trapped in the same policy architecture, still spraying the same chemicals, still watching water tables drop. The Cancer Train still departs at 9:30 PM. What has changed is that the evidence is now overwhelming, the alternatives are proven, and the question is purely one of political will.

Geography · Punjab

The Three Regions of Punjab

Punjab's cancer burden is not uniform. The state divides into three ecological and cultural zones, each with a different relationship to the Green Revolution's inputs and harms.

Malwa — The Cancer Belt

Southern Punjab, formerly forested. Highest pesticide use in state. Cotton-growing zone where endosulfan, monocrotophos and DDT were applied most heavily. Home to Bathinda and Sangrur — districts with documented uranium contamination. Cancer rate: 1,089/million vs national 800/million.

Doaba — The Transition Zone

Central belt between the Beas and Sutlej rivers. Moderate cancer incidence. Significant pesticide use but more vegetable cultivation has preserved some agrobiodiversity. Heavy NRI remittances fund farm inputs — paradoxically intensifying chemical use.

Majha — The Buffer Zone

Northern Punjab, bordering Pakistan. Lower cancer rates but facing the same groundwater depletion crisis. Wheat and rice monocultures dominate. Less industrial pesticide history due to cooler temperatures reducing pest pressure, but soil degradation equally severe.
